# Social Engineering Attacks

## 1. Introduction
Social engineering is a cybersecurity technique in which attackers manipulate people into sharing confidential information or performing actions that may compromise security. Instead of targeting only technical weaknesses, these attacks exploit human trust, fear, curiosity, or urgency.

## 2. Common Types of Social Engineering Attacks

### 2.1 Phishing
Phishing uses fake emails, messages, or websites to trick people into revealing sensitive information.

**Example:** A fraudulent email asks a user to verify their account through a suspicious link.

**Prevention:** Check the sender's address, avoid suspicious links, and verify requests through official channels.

### 2.2 Spear Phishing
Spear phishing is a targeted form of phishing that uses information about a particular person or organization to make a message appear genuine.

**Example:** An employee receives a fake message that appears to come from their manager.

**Prevention:** Verify unusual requests, even when the sender appears familiar, and use multi-factor authentication.

### 2.3 Pretexting
Pretexting occurs when an attacker invents a false identity or situation to convince someone to share information.

**Example:** Someone pretends to be technical support and asks an employee for confidential account details.

**Prevention:** Confirm the person's identity and follow official information-sharing procedures.

### 2.4 Baiting
Baiting uses an attractive offer or item to persuade someone to take an unsafe action.

**Example:** A person finds an unknown USB drive and connects it to a computer, potentially exposing the system to malware.

**Prevention:** Never connect unknown storage devices, and avoid untrusted downloads or offers.

### 2.5 Quid Pro Quo
Quid pro quo is an attack in which someone offers a benefit or service in exchange for information or access.

**Example:** A fake support representative offers assistance in exchange for a password.

**Prevention:** Use verified support channels and never share passwords with anyone.

### 2.6 Tailgating
Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted physical area.

**Example:** Someone enters an office building by following an employee through a secured door.

**Prevention:** Follow access-control rules, use authorized entry methods, and report suspicious activity.

## 3. Effects of Social Engineering
- Theft of confidential or personal information.
- Unauthorized access to accounts and systems.
- Financial losses.
- Installation of malicious software.
- Damage to an organization's reputation.
- Loss of trust among customers and employees.

## 4. Prevention and Protection
- Be cautious with unexpected emails, calls, and messages.
- Verify identities before sharing information.
- Never share passwords or one-time passwords (OTPs).
- Enable multi-factor authentication.
- Use strong, unique passwords.
- Report suspicious messages to the appropriate security team.
- Provide regular cybersecurity awareness training.
- Follow physical security and access-control policies.

## 5. What to Do If an Attack Is Suspected
- Stop interacting with the suspicious message or caller.
- Report the incident to the appropriate support or security team.
- If account details were shared, change the affected password using the official website or app.
- Enable multi-factor authentication if available.
- Follow the organization's incident-response procedures.

## 6. Conclusion
Social engineering attacks exploit human behavior to compromise security. Learning to recognize suspicious requests, verifying identities, and following safe security practices can help individuals and organizations reduce these risks.
